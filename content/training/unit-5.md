# 5 - Your Map and Your Courses

---
phase: 2
minutes: 45
deliverable: A first course on your own map, with your checkpoints listed
---

## Page

Do this unit when your map arrives, or when the mapper sends you a draft to check. If you don't have a map yet, keep teaching the kit activities, which don't need one, and come back here later.

**Checking the draft.** If you're the Eyes on the Ground for your map, the mapper will send you a draft and some questions. Walk the area with the draft in your hand, and turn it so it matches what's in front of you. Look for anything missing, anything that has changed (a new fence, a removed play structure), and anything students couldn't recognize. Mark it on a printout or a photo and send it back. That walk is also the best preparation for designing courses, because you'll see your grounds the way the map shows them.

**Reading the map.** School orienteering maps use the sprint map symbols (ISSprOM): buildings are dark gray, paved areas are light brown, open grass is yellow, and black shows fences, walls, paths and other built features. Olive green marks areas that are always out of bounds, such as private gardens, and purple is for course markings. [Map Symbols](/lessons/school/grade-3-5/lesson-4/) and [Map Discussion](/activities/core/map-discussion/) show how students learn them. The [Maps](/reference/equipment/maps/) page explains printing and course drawing.

**Designing a first course.** A good first course for students is easy to succeed at:

- Put each checkpoint on a feature students can tell apart on the map and on the ground: a building corner, a bench, a fence end, a tree on its own. Avoid "somewhere in the field."
- Keep every checkpoint inside the boundary and in sight of where you'll stand, at first.
- Mark out-of-bounds areas on the map, and say them out loud in the briefing.
- For a [Score-O](/activities/core/score-o/), spread 10 to 15 checkpoints so there are easy ones near the start. For a [Point-to-Point](/activities/core/point-to-point/), keep the legs short and the route obvious.
- Print the map at the scale the mapper gave you (usually 1:2,000 to 1:4,000 for a school), so distances on paper match the ground.

You can draw the course by hand on a printed map with a red or purple pen. [Purple Pen](https://purplepen.golde.org/) is free course design software for Windows if you'd like printed course maps.

## What you'll make

A **first course** on your own map: what kind it is (Score-O or Point-to-Point), the start, and each checkpoint with the feature it's on. Add a photo of the map with your circles drawn on it if you can. A reviewer looks at it with you before you use it with a class.

## What we look for

- Every checkpoint is on a feature students can recognize on the map and on the ground
- All checkpoints are inside the boundary, with out-of-bounds areas marked
- The first checkpoints are easy to find from the start
- The course fits the class length, with time for the briefing and reflection
- The map is printed to scale

## Opening

Your map is here, or at least a draft of it. Congratulations, that's a big step.

Where are you with it: checking a draft for the mapper, or ready to design a course for your students?

## Tutor

Your job in this unit is to help the teacher check their map (if it's a draft) and design one first course on it that their students can succeed at. Use the teacher's unit plan, if you have it, to see which class the course is for.

1. Find out where they are: a draft to check, a finished map, or no map yet. If no map yet, say that's fine, suggest kit activities from their plan in the meantime, and offer to help with anything else about the map (for example, how to answer the mapper's questions). Then stop.
2. Draft to check: suggest they walk the area with the draft turned to match the ground, and ask what they've found so far. Help them describe changes clearly for the mapper ("the bench by the east door is gone"). Don't guess at what the mapper wants beyond that; Navigation Games and the mapper answer those questions.
3. Course design: ask which class it's for and which kind of course (Score-O or Point-to-Point), then ask them to name the features they'd use for checkpoints, one or two at a time. For each, ask whether a student could find it on the map and recognize it on the ground. If a checkpoint is vague ("in the field"), ask what feature it could go on instead. Ask about out-of-bounds areas and where they'll stand.
4. Check the timing against the class length, including the briefing and a reflection at the end.
5. If they want printed course maps, mention Purple Pen and the [Maps](/reference/equipment/maps/) page. Hand-drawn is fine.
6. Ask them to share a photo of the map with their circles, if they can, by replying to the email they get when they send the course in.
7. Write the course in a fenced code block labeled `deliverable`, in this format:

```
Course for: (class and date)
Kind: (Score-O or Point-to-Point)
Start:
Checkpoints: (number, feature, and any note, one per line)
Out of bounds:
Map printed at: (scale)
Questions for Navigation Games:
```

8. Tell them to press "Send to Navigation Games" under it, and that someone will look at it with them before they use it with a class.

Rubric:

- Every checkpoint on a recognizable feature
- All checkpoints inside the boundary, out-of-bounds marked
- Easy first checkpoints
- Fits the class length
- Printed to scale

## Reviewer notes

About 15 minutes. Check the checkpoint features against the map if they sent a photo, and look for anything unsafe (streets, parking lots, water, blind corners). If the map is a draft, note the teacher's corrections for the mapper and the YMP committee. Reply within a week, or before the class the course is for if that's sooner.
