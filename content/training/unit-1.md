# 1 - What Orienteering Is and Why It Works

## Page

Orienteering is a sport in which you use a map to visit checkpoints. The map shows where each checkpoint is. How you get there is up to you, because there is no marked route. Deciding where to go, choosing a way and noticing when you have gone wrong are the heart of the game. Read [About Orienteering](/about/about-orienteering/) for the full picture.

Three ideas run through the whole curriculum:

- **[Checking](/reference/frameworks/skill-concept-sequence/#checking).** Confirm before moving on: am I at the right checkpoint, and do I know where I'm going next? Partners check each other, then students check themselves. This is also how the lessons work without electronic timing.
- **[Growth mindset](/reference/frameworks/skill-concept-sequence/#growth-mindset).** Many people believe they are "bad with directions." The lessons make that belief hard to keep: each step is small and achievable, accuracy counts more than speed, and everyone does the same activities.
- **[Being lost](/reference/frameworks/skill-concept-sequence/#being-lost).** Getting lost is a feeling as well as a navigation problem. The curriculum prepares students for it: small bounded spaces first, safety routines before independence, and a plan for what to do when you are confused.

The lessons follow the [experiential learning cycle](/reference/frameworks/experiential-learning/): do the activity, think about what happened, draw out the idea, and try again. That is why reflection questions sit inside each lesson, not only at the end. Orienteering also builds [social-emotional skills](/reference/frameworks/social-emotional-learning/) such as independence, persistence and working with a partner.

## What you'll make

A **three-sentence pitch** for orienteering, written for someone at your school who hasn't heard of it: your principal, a parent or a colleague.

## What we look for

- Says what orienteering is: using a map to find checkpoints, choosing your own route
- Names at least one benefit for students that matters to your listener (physical activity, spatial thinking, confidence, teamwork)
- Names one life skill it builds, such as handling being lost or making decisions
- Uses plain language with no unexplained orienteering terms
- Three sentences, or close to it

## Opening

In this unit we'll practice explaining orienteering to someone who has never heard of it. First, a quick check: after reading the page, how would you describe orienteering in one sentence?

## Tutor

Your job is to help the teacher understand the core ideas of the curriculum and write a short pitch.

1. Ask for their one-sentence description of orienteering. Gently correct misunderstandings (for example, that it is mainly about compasses, or that it is a race).
2. Ask which of the three ideas (checking, growth mindset, being lost) seems most important for their students, and why. Connect their answer to their situation from the learner profile, if one is provided.
3. Then role-play. Say you will play a skeptical listener, ask who it should be (principal, parent or colleague), and stay in character for two or three exchanges. Raise realistic concerns: time away from other PE units, safety, cost, "kids will just run around." Then step out of character and say you are the tutor again.
4. Ask the teacher to write their three-sentence pitch. Give feedback against the rubric below, one or two specific suggestions at a time. Let them revise.
5. When they are happy with it, repeat the final pitch back in a fenced code block labeled `deliverable`, and tell them to send it in.

Rubric:

- Says what orienteering is: using a map to find checkpoints, choosing your own route
- Names at least one benefit that matters to the chosen listener
- Names one life skill
- Plain language, no unexplained jargon (if they write "controls," suggest "checkpoints")
- About three sentences

## Reviewer notes

About 5 minutes. Check that the pitch is accurate and in plain language. A strong pitch can be shared (with permission) as an example for future cohorts.
