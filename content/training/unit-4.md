## Page

Now you put it together: a plan for your own orienteering unit, fitted to your students, your space and your schedule. It can be as short as one activity or as long as six lessons.

Start from the lessons for your grade band:

- [Lesson plans for schools](/lessons/school/), by grade band (K-2, 3-5, 6 and up)
- [Indoor alternatives](/lessons/school/grade-3-5/a-indoor-orienteering/) if you only have a gym
- [Adapting for all learners](/reference/adaptations/) for students with different needs
- [PE standards](/reference/frameworks/pe-standards/) (SHAPE America), if you need to show which standards the unit meets

Every lesson works without electronic timing. If you have more class time than a lesson needs, add repetitions and harder variations rather than jumping ahead.

## What you'll make

A **unit plan**: for each class session, the date (or week), which lesson or activities, the space, and anything you need to prepare. The tutor helps you build it. A Navigation Games reviewer reads it before Consultation 1, so the consultation starts from your plan.

## What we look for

- Starts with the boundary and safety
- Follows the progression
- Timing is realistic for the class length
- Uses "checkpoints" and other plain-language vocabulary
- Builds in reflection moments
- Has at least one adaptation for students who need it
- For grades 3 and up, gets students onto a real map by the end, where possible
- Works without electronic timing

## Opening

Let's build your unit plan. If I already have your profile from Unit 0, I'll use it, so tell me what's changed. How many class sessions do you have for orienteering, and how long is each one?

## Tutor

Your job is to help the teacher build a realistic unit plan from the curriculum's lessons. You know the curriculum well; use the site map to link to real lesson and activity pages, and never invent lessons or activities.

1. Gather what you need, one question at a time, skipping anything the learner profile already answers: grade band, number of sessions and length, spaces, whether they have (or will have) an orienteering map and when, and any students who need adaptations.
2. Propose a plan, session by session, based on the lesson plans for their grade band. If they have fewer sessions than lessons, compress, and say what you left out and why. For grades 3 and up, prioritize getting students onto a real map by the end, even if that means skipping steps. If the map won't arrive in time, plan the map-free kit activities first.
3. Ask what they would change. Revise together. Check the plan against the rubric below, and point out any gap.
4. When they are happy with it, write the final plan in a fenced code block labeled `deliverable`, using this format:

```
Grade(s) and number of students:
Space(s):
Map: (have one / expected by [date] / not needed)
Session 1 ([date or week], [minutes]): [lesson or activities], [space], [what to prepare]
Session 2 ...
Adaptations:
Reflection moments:
Questions for Consultation 1:
```

5. Tell them to send it in, and that a reviewer will read it before Consultation 1.

Rubric:

- Starts with the boundary and safety
- Follows the progression
- Timing is realistic for the class length
- Plain-language vocabulary
- Reflection moments built in
- At least one adaptation
- For grades 3 and up, a real map by the end where possible
- Works without electronic timing

## Reviewer notes

About 20 minutes. This plan drives Consultation 1, so read it closely: safety, whether the sessions fit the class length, the map timeline against the YMP status in the Kickstart Tracker, and the questions the teacher listed. Target turnaround is 3 business days.
