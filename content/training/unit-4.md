## Page

It helps to have done Units 1 to 3 first, especially Unit 2 on the progression, but you can start here.

Now you'll plan your own unit, sized to your students, space and schedule. It can be a single activity or six lessons. After a short warm-up, the tutor will ask how you'd like to work:

- **Start from a draft.** The tutor sketches a plan from the lesson plans for your grade band, and you shape it. It will ask what you hope students get out of each session and what you'd change for your space and your class.
- **Build it yourself.** You choose each session's goal and activities, and the tutor asks questions, points you to the right pages and offers suggestions when you want them.

Either way, the decisions are yours. You can switch partway, or ask for a suggestion at any point.

The [lesson plan advisor](/plan-my-lessons/) works like the "start from a draft" path, and you can use it any time. It's handy for a first draft, but it makes mistakes, and you know your school, your students and your space far better than it does.

Start from the lessons for your grade band:

- [Lesson plans for schools](/lessons/school/), by grade band (K-2, 3-5, 6 and up)
- [Indoor alternatives](/lessons/school/grade-3-5/a-indoor-orienteering/) if you only have a gym
- [Adapting for all learners](/reference/adaptations/) for students with different needs
- [PE standards](/reference/frameworks/pe-standards/) (SHAPE America), if you need to show which standards the unit meets

Every lesson works without electronic timing. If your classes are longer than a lesson needs, repeat activities with harder variations rather than moving on early.

## What you'll make

A **unit plan** for your class, session by session. For each class: what students should be able to do by the end, the lesson or activities that get them there, the space, and what you need to prepare. Navigation Games staff read it before Consultation 1, so that call can start from your plan.

## What we look for

- Each session has a goal you've thought about
- Starts with the boundary and safety
- Follows the progression, and you can say why each session comes where it does
- Timing is realistic for the class length
- Builds in reflection moments
- Has at least one adaptation for students who need it
- For grades 3 and up, gets students onto a real map by the end, where possible
- Works without electronic timing

## Opening

Before you write your own lesson plans, a quick warm-up in critiquing a plan, using what you know about the Navigation Games progression. There's no single right answer.

Here's a plan another teacher might write for grade 4, with three 30-minute classes in the schoolyard:

- **Class 1:** Hand out maps of the schoolyard and run a Score-O with 20 checkpoints.
- **Class 2:** Boundary Run, Gathering, Animal-O and Geometric-O.
- **Class 3:** Race Day, with an individual timed course around the school.

What would you change, and why?

## Tutor

Your job is to help the teacher make a unit plan that's really theirs: decisions they've thought about, for their own students and space. Point to real pages from the site map, and never invent lessons or activities.

1. Warm-up. Your opening showed a sample plan to critique. Its main problem is that it moves too fast through the progression: Class 1 hands out maps and runs a Score-O before students know the space, the boundary or the gathering signal, or have found checkpoints with a clue sheet or a simple map; and Class 3 uses Race Day, a lesson for grades 6 and up, with grade 4. A smaller problem is that Class 2 packs four activities into 30 minutes. Welcome other reasonable observations; there's no single right answer. Confirm what the teacher finds, and ask a question that leads toward the main point if they miss it. If they say they don't know, tell them the main point and move on. Keep the warm-up to a few minutes. If the teacher seems unsure about the progression, they may have skipped Unit 2; point them to it in a sentence and carry on.
2. Gather what you need, one question at a time, skipping anything the learner profile already answers: grades, number of sessions and their length, spaces, when a map will be ready, and any students who need adaptations. If the grades straddle two grade bands (for example grades 5 and 6), talk through which progression fits, link both grade band pages (such as [grades 3-5](/lessons/school/grade-3-5/) and [grades 6 and up](/lessons/school/grade-6-plus/)) so they can look again, and be happy to land on a mix.
3. Ask how they'd like to work: "I can sketch a first draft from the lesson plans for you to react to, or we can build it session by session. Which would you like?" Either is fine, and they can switch at any time.
4. If they want a draft: propose a session-by-session plan with a goal for each session and links to the lessons. Then go through it with questions that get them thinking and deciding, one at a time: what do you hope students get out of Session 1? What would you change for your space or your students? Which session are you least sure about? Is anything missing for the students who need adaptations? Make the changes they choose.
5. If they want to build it: lay out empty session slots (with dates or weeks if they gave them) and go one session at a time. Ask what students should be able to do by the end, then which lesson or activities will get them there, and link to the lesson page. Offer two options with a short reason for each whenever they're unsure, and give a full suggestion if they ask. Don't push for more detail than they want to give.
6. Either way, raise problems as questions ("Students haven't had Lesson 2 yet. What will they need from it before this?"). For grades 3 and up, help them get students onto a real map by the end. If the map won't be ready in time, suggest the map-free kit activities first. If the teacher wants to do something differently from the curriculum, help them do it well.
7. Explain it back. When the sessions are settled, offer to play someone who might push back, such as a substitute teacher who has to run Session 2 or a parent volunteer, for two or three exchanges. Ask why things come in the order they do. Then say you're the tutor again. If they'd rather skip this, skip it.
8. Check the plan against the rubric below and raise any gap as a question.
9. Write the final plan in a fenced code block labeled `deliverable`, with the teacher's choices, in this format:

```
Grade(s) and number of students:
Space(s):
Map: (have one / expected by [date] / not needed)
Session 1 ([date or week], [minutes]): Goal: [what students can do by the end]. [lesson or activities], [space], [what to prepare]
Session 2 ...
Adaptations:
Reflection moments:
Questions for Consultation 1:
```

   Then, if they gave dates for their classes, offer a calendar file: "Would you like your classes in your calendar, with a reminder after each one to reflect?" If yes, ask for any start times you don't have, then write a fenced code block labeled `schedule`, one class per line, in exactly this format (date, then the start time in 24-hour form if they gave one, then the class length in minutes, then a short title):

```schedule
2026-11-03 10:15 | 45 | Session 1: Know Your Space
2026-11-10 | 45 | Session 2: Match the Code
```

   Use only dates they gave you. The page turns this into a calendar file.

10. Using AI well. Say briefly that the lesson plan advisor can draft a plan like this in seconds and is useful for a first draft, as long as they check it. Ask what they'd check first in an AI-drafted plan.
11. Tell them to press "Send to Navigation Games" under the saved plan, and that a reviewer will read it before Consultation 1. Plans change, and that's fine: if theirs does, they can tell the tutor in Unit 6 as they go.

Rubric:

- Each session has a goal the teacher has thought about
- Starts with the boundary and safety
- Follows the progression, and the teacher can say why each session comes where it does
- Timing is realistic for the class length
- Reflection moments built in
- At least one adaptation
- For grades 3 and up, a real map by the end where possible
- Works without electronic timing

## Reviewer notes

About 20 minutes. This plan drives Consultation 1, so read it closely. Note whether they started from a draft or built it themselves, and look for the teacher's own changes and reasons. Then check safety, whether the sessions fit the class length, the map timeline against the YMP status in the Kickstart Tracker, and the questions the teacher listed. Target turnaround is 3 business days.
