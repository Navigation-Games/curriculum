## Page

Orienteering is a sport where you use a map to visit checkpoints. How you get from one to the next is up to you, because there's no marked route.

**Start with the tutor below.** It takes you through three things and gives you the links as you need them:

1. **Your nearest club and its next event** (about 5 minutes). It's fine if you can't go. We want you to know where to find events.
2. **A three-sentence pitch** for someone at your school who has never heard of orienteering (about 15 minutes).
3. **A course you design and test**, at home or in your neighborhood (about 20 minutes). You leave the conversation to do it, and pick up where you left off when you're back.

The background below is there if you want more.

<details>
<summary>Find orienteering near you</summary>

- **Your local club.** Type your zip code into Orienteering USA's [club finder](https://orienteeringusa.org/events/clubs/), then look at your nearest club's website for upcoming events. Local events usually have a beginner course.
- **Permanent courses.** A permanent course has checkpoints that stay in place all year, often in a park, so you can print the map and go whenever you like. Look on OUSA's [Permanent & DIY Courses](https://orienteeringusa.org/events/permanent-courses/) page and on your club's website.
- **More events.** Attackpoint has an [event map](https://www.attackpoint.org/eventmap.jsp) and an [event list](https://www.attackpoint.org/events.jsp) from clubs all over. National and other major events are on the [OUSA calendar](https://orienteeringusa.org/events/calendar/).

If you can go to an event, please do! A beginner course at a local event is the quickest way to get to know the sport and learn how it feels to do a course.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Design a course and test it</summary>

Pick one:

- **At home.** Sketch a map of your home, room by room, with doors and big furniture. Draw a course on it with five checkpoints ([Drawing courses on maps](/reference/equipment/maps/#drawing-courses) shows how), and put a sticky note with a letter at each spot. Then follow your map from 1 to 5 and write down the letters. Better still, hand the map to someone at home and watch them do it.
- **In your neighborhood.** Print a map of your block from Google Maps, or make an orienteering-style one on [OpenOrienteeringMap](https://oomap.dna-software.co.uk/). Draw a course with five checkpoints on features you can find, like a corner, a bench or a big tree, and walk it. This video shows how to make a course map of your neighborhood with OpenOrienteeringMap:

<YouTube id="ebV9WsbGNpI" title="Making a course map of your neighborhood with OpenOrienteeringMap" />

Notice what was hard, whether it was drawing the map, choosing the checkpoints or following it.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What orienteering is, and why it's good for kids</summary>

[About Orienteering](/about/about-orienteering/) explains the sport, with two short videos (competitive orienteering, and kids doing Animal-O at a camp). [Our Impact](/about/impact/) has our list of benefits to children. Think about what you think the greatest benefits of learning orienteering are.

Three of the things the lessons build:

- **[Checking](/reference/frameworks/skill-concept-sequence/#checking).** Students learn to confirm they're at the right checkpoint by matching a picture or code to their clue sheet, first with a partner checking them and then on their own. In competition, electronic timing is used to confirm the athletes visited the correct checkpoints in the correct order, but the athletes first check the code at each checkpoint themselves.
- **[Growth mindset](/reference/frameworks/skill-concept-sequence/#growth-mindset).** Growth mindset is believing you can learn and get better at something, as opposed to believing you can't. We build that belief with small, achievable steps: each step in the curriculum is small enough to succeed at. By the time students navigate a course on a real orienteering map, they've built up confidence from many small successes along the way.
- **[Handling being lost](/reference/frameworks/skill-concept-sequence/#being-lost).** Feeling lost or confused is normal, and the first step is to calm down enough to think. Students practice a simple routine for those moments: stop, turn the map to match the ground, and look for a feature they recognize. Mistakes are welcome, because they teach you how to read a map better.

The lessons follow the [experiential learning cycle](/reference/frameworks/experiential-learning/), where students try something, reflect on what happened, and analyze it. Orienteering also builds [social-emotional skills](/reference/frameworks/social-emotional-learning/) such as independence and working with a partner.

</details>

## Assignments

- **Your nearest club and its next event.**
- **A three-sentence pitch** for orienteering, for someone at your school who has never heard of it. Your principal, a parent or a colleague would all work.
- **Your course:** what you made, and what you noticed when you or someone else did it.

## What we look for in the pitch

- Says what orienteering is: finding checkpoints with a map and choosing your own route
- Gives a benefit your listener cares about
- Mentions a life skill it builds, like handling being lost or making decisions
- Uses plain words, with any orienteering terms explained
- Is about three sentences long

## Opening

Let's start with orienteering near you. Type your zip code into Orienteering USA's [club finder](https://orienteeringusa.org/events/clubs/) and look at your nearest club's website. What's the club, and when is its next event?

## Tutor

Your job is to get the teacher through three things quickly: their nearest club's next event, a three-sentence pitch, and a course they design and test. Keep every message short.

1. Club. Take whatever they found, and never name or guess a club, event or course yourself, because you can't look them up. If they found nothing nearby, take that as a fine answer and mention the Attackpoint [event map](https://www.attackpoint.org/eventmap.jsp) and OUSA's [Permanent & DIY Courses](https://orienteeringusa.org/events/permanent-courses/) page in one sentence. If they might go to an event, encourage it in a few words and suggest the beginner course. Then move on.
2. Pitch. Ask who it's for (their principal, a parent, a colleague or someone else) and which one benefit matters most for their students; [Our Impact](/about/impact/) has a list if they want ideas. Then ask them to write three sentences for that person. Give feedback against the rubric below, one or two suggestions at most, and let them revise. If the pitch is good, say so in a few words and move on. If they're stuck, give them a draft to edit.
3. Course. Ask them to design a short course at home or in their neighborhood and test it, using the "Design a course and test it" section on the page (link [Drawing courses on maps](/reference/equipment/maps/#drawing-courses); for a neighborhood course, also link the [OpenOrienteeringMap video](https://youtu.be/ebV9WsbGNpI)). Say it takes about 20 minutes and they can come back to this conversation when they're done. When they're back, ask what was hard or surprising. Connect their answer to their students in one or two sentences (for example, if following their own map was harder than expected, that is what students feel, and it's why the lessons start before any map and add one skill at a time).
4. When all three are done, write a fenced code block labeled `deliverable` with exactly these lines, and tell them to press "Send to Navigation Games" under it:

```deliverable
Nearest club and its next event:
Pitch for: (for example, my principal)
Pitch:
My course (where, and what I noticed):
```

5. Only if the learner profile's Map line starts with "Applying to YMP": after the deliverable, ask once, "One more thing: did you submit the YMP application?" If yes, say someone from the YMP committee will be in touch, which can take a few days. If not yet, say it only takes a few minutes with the box from Unit 1. Take any answer. Don't ask about it at all if the Map line says anything else.

Rubric:

- Says what orienteering is: using a map to find checkpoints, choosing your own route
- Names at least one benefit that matters to the chosen listener
- Names one life skill
- Plain language, with any orienteering terms explained
- About three sentences

## Reviewer notes

About 5 minutes. Check that the pitch is accurate and in plain language. Their nearest club may be a local partner for their school; if it isn't in the Tracker's Local partners tab, consider adding it. A strong pitch can be shared (with permission) as an example for future cohorts. If the teacher is applying to YMP, read their answer to the tutor's question about the application too, and set the Tracker's YMP applied date once Orienteering USA confirms it.
