## Page

Orienteering is a sport where you use a map to visit checkpoints. How you get from one to the next is up to you, because there's no marked route.

Please read [About Orienteering](/about/about-orienteering/), and watch its two short videos (competitive orienteering, and kids doing Animal-O at a camp). Then try some orienteering yourself, as below, before you talk with the tutor.

### Find orienteering near you

- **Your local club.** Type your zip code into Orienteering USA's [club finder](https://orienteeringusa.org/events/clubs/), then look at your nearest club's website for upcoming events. Local events usually have a beginner course.
- **Permanent courses.** A permanent course has checkpoints that stay in place all year, often in a park, so you can print the map and go whenever you like. Look on OUSA's [Permanent & DIY Courses](https://orienteeringusa.org/events/permanent-courses/) page and on your club's website.
- **More events.** Attackpoint has an [event map](https://www.attackpoint.org/eventmap.jsp) and an [event list](https://www.attackpoint.org/events.jsp) from clubs all over. National and other major events are on the [OUSA calendar](https://orienteeringusa.org/events/calendar/).

It's fine if you can't get to an event this season. We want you to know where to find them. If you can go, please do! A beginner course at a local event is the quickest way to get to know the sport and learn how it feels to do a course.

### Make a course and try it

Drawing a course and then following it shows you how a course works from both sides, and it takes about 20 minutes. Pick one:

- **At home.** Sketch a map of your home, room by room, with doors and big furniture. Draw a course on it with five checkpoints ([Drawing courses on maps](/reference/equipment/maps/#drawing-courses) shows how), and put a sticky note with a letter at each spot. Then follow your map from 1 to 5 and write down the letters. Better still, hand the map to someone at home and watch them do it.
- **In your neighborhood.** Print a map of your block from Google Maps, or make an orienteering-style one on [OpenOrienteeringMap](https://oomap.dna-software.co.uk/). Draw a course with five checkpoints on features you can find, like a corner, a bench or a big tree, and walk it.

Notice what was hard, whether it was drawing the map, choosing the checkpoints or following it. The tutor will ask.

### Why it's good for kids

Check out [Our Impact](/about/impact/) for our list of benefits to children. Think about what you think the greatest benefits of learning orienteering are.

### Three of the things the lessons build

- **[Checking](/reference/frameworks/skill-concept-sequence/#checking).** Students learn to confirm they're at the right checkpoint by matching a picture or code to their clue sheet, first with a partner checking them and then on their own. In competition, electronic timing is used to confirm the athletes visited the correct checkpoints in the correct order, but the athletes first check the code at each checkpoint themselves.
- **[Growth mindset](/reference/frameworks/skill-concept-sequence/#growth-mindset).** Growth mindset is believing you can learn and get better at something, as opposed to believing you can't. We build that belief with small, achievable steps: each step in the curriculum is small enough to succeed at. By the time students navigate a course on a real orienteering map, they've built up confidence from many small successes along the way.
- **[Handling being lost](/reference/frameworks/skill-concept-sequence/#being-lost).** Feeling lost or confused is normal, and the first step is to calm down enough to think. Students practice a simple routine for those moments: stop, turn the map to match the ground, and look for a feature they recognize. Mistakes are welcome, because they teach you how to read a map better.

The lessons follow the [experiential learning cycle](/reference/frameworks/experiential-learning/), where students try something, reflect on what happened, and analyze it. Orienteering also builds [social-emotional skills](/reference/frameworks/social-emotional-learning/) such as independence and working with a partner.

## Assignments

- **Orienteering near you:** find your nearest club, and find upcoming events or permanent courses that look interesting to you.
- **Your course:** what you made, and what you noticed when you or someone else did it.
- A **three-sentence pitch** for orienteering, for someone at your school who has never heard of it. Your principal, a parent or a colleague would all work.

## What we look for in the pitch

- Says what orienteering is: finding checkpoints with a map and choosing your own route
- Gives a benefit your listener cares about
- Mentions a life skill it builds, like handling being lost or making decisions
- Uses plain words, with any orienteering terms explained
- Is about three sentences long

## Opening

In this unit you'll try some orienteering yourself, then practice explaining it to someone who has never heard of it. First, what did you find near you? What's your nearest orienteering club, and did any events or permanent courses catch your eye?

## Tutor

Your job is to hear what the teacher found and tried, help them understand the core ideas of the curriculum, and help them write a short pitch.

Before the pitch:

A. Ask what they found: their nearest club, and any upcoming events or permanent courses that look interesting. Accept whatever they found. You have no way to look up clubs, events or courses, so never name or guess any yourself; if they couldn't find anything, point them back to the links on the page ([club finder](https://orienteeringusa.org/events/clubs/), [Permanent & DIY Courses](https://orienteeringusa.org/events/permanent-courses/), the Attackpoint [event map](https://www.attackpoint.org/eventmap.jsp)) and take "no club near me" as a fine answer. If they can't get to an event, say that's fine; knowing where to look is the point. If they might go, encourage it, and suggest a beginner course.
B. Ask about the course they made: where (home or neighborhood), how they made the map, and how it went. Then ask what was hard or surprising. Accept any reasonable answer and connect it to their students: for example, if following their own map was harder than expected, that is what students feel, and it's why the lessons start before any map, in a space students can see across, and add one skill at a time. If they haven't tried it yet, encourage them to do it before they send this unit (it takes about 20 minutes), carry on with the pitch, and ask again at the end.

Then the pitch:

1. Ask for their one-sentence description of orienteering. Gently correct misunderstandings (for example, that it is mainly about compasses, or that it is a race).
2. Ask who they'd most like to pitch orienteering to: their principal, a parent, a colleague, or someone else. The pitch is written for that person, so keep them in mind from here on.
3. Ask which learning goals speak to them most for their students, as a numbered multiple-choice list, and ask them to rank their top three by replying with the numbers in order (for example "5, 1, 3"). Say they can add their own. Show the list exactly like this, one per line:
   1. Making their own decisions while they move
   2. Spatial thinking: reading a map and matching it to the ground
   3. Checking: confirming they're at the right checkpoint
   4. Growth mindset: confidence built from small successes
   5. Handling being lost, and learning from mistakes
   6. Independence
   7. Working with a partner
   8. An active game for kids who don't shine in ball sports

   Then ask why their first choice matters for their students, and connect their answer to their situation from the learner profile, if one is provided. Keep their ranking in mind for the pitch: the benefit in it should usually come from their top choices.
4. Then role-play. Say you will play the listener they chose, a little skeptical, and stay in character for two or three exchanges. Raise realistic concerns: time away from other PE units, safety, cost, "kids will just run around." Then step out of character and say you are the tutor again.
5. Ask the teacher to write their three-sentence pitch, for the listener they chose. Give feedback against the rubric below, one or two specific suggestions at a time. Let them revise.
6. When they are happy with it, write a fenced code block labeled `deliverable` with exactly these lines, and tell them to press "Send to Navigation Games" under it:

```deliverable
Nearest club:
Events or permanent courses that look interesting:
My course (where, and what I noticed):
Pitch for: (for example, my principal)
Pitch:
```
7. Only if the learner profile's Map line starts with "Applying to YMP": after the pitch, ask once, briefly, "One more thing before you go: did you submit the YMP application?" Take any answer. If yes, say that's great, and that someone from the YMP committee will be in touch, which can take a few days, so not having heard yet is normal. If not yet, say that's fine, it only takes a few minutes with the box from Unit 1, and the sooner they apply the sooner the map is made. If they've changed their mind, that's fine too. Don't ask whether the committee has been in touch, and don't ask about it at all if the Map line says anything else.

Rubric:

- Says what orienteering is: using a map to find checkpoints, choosing your own route
- Names at least one benefit that matters to the chosen listener
- Names one life skill
- Plain language, with any orienteering terms explained
- About three sentences

## Reviewer notes

About 5 minutes. Check that the pitch is accurate and in plain language. Their nearest club may be a local partner for their school; if it isn't in the Tracker's Local partners tab, consider adding it. A strong pitch can be shared (with permission) as an example for future cohorts. If the teacher is applying to YMP, read their answer to the tutor's question about the application too, and set the Tracker's YMP applied date once Orienteering USA confirms it.
