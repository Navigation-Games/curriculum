# Video Script: Lesson 2 - Return

**Duration target:** 5-6 minutes
**Audience:** PE teachers

## Opening (visual: title card)

Lesson 2 builds on the boundary from Lesson 1. Now students learn to navigate back to a central home base from anywhere in the space. This is the first step toward independent navigation: knowing where you are and how to get back.

## What You'll Need (visual: materials laid out)

- Whistle or flag (gathering signal)
- Cone or marker for home base
- A gym, schoolyard, or local park with an established boundary
- Set up a clear home base marker in the center of the space before class

## Learning Goals (visual: goals on screen)

### Orienteering Goals
- Know where home base (start/finish) is from anywhere in the boundary
- Navigate back to home base from any position in the area
- Develop spatial awareness and a sense of direction within the space
- Respond quickly to the gathering signal as practice for returning

### PE Standards (SHAPE America)
- Demonstrate locomotor skills (S1.E1, S1.E2)
- Engage actively in class (S3.E2)
- Work cooperatively with others (S4.E5)

## Lesson Flow (visual: timeline graphic showing segments)

Plan for 15 to 30 minutes. About 3 minutes for the Boundary Run review, 5 minutes for the pointing exercise, 10 to 15 minutes for Gathering rounds, and 2 minutes for reflection.

## Delivery (visual: demonstration with students)

### Segment 1: Boundary Run Review (3 min)

[SHOW: teacher jogging the boundary with students, then stopping at a cone in the center]

Start with a quick Boundary Run to remind students of the space. Then gather everyone at the center and introduce home base. Place a flag or tall cone so it's visible from most of the area.

"This is home base. In orienteering, this is your start and your finish. No matter where you go, you always come back here."

### Segment 2: Pointing Exercise (5 min)

[SHOW: students walking to corners and edges of the space, then turning and pointing toward home base]

Send students to walk to different spots: corners, edges, behind a tree, near the fence. From each spot, ask them to point to home base. Can they see it? Can they point even when they can't see it directly?

[SHOW: close-up of students pointing in different directions from various positions]

This builds spatial awareness. Students begin to track their position relative to a fixed point.

### Segment 3: Gathering (10-15 min)

[SHOW: students spread out across the space, teacher blows whistle, students run back to home base]

Now introduce the Gathering activity. Students move freely within the boundary. When you blow the whistle (or raise the flag), everyone returns to home base as fast as they can.

Time the group. "That took 22 seconds. Can you beat that?"

[SHOW: students spread out further, whistle blows, students running back]

Repeat several rounds. Each time, encourage students to spread out further. They'll naturally start from the farthest spots as the challenge grows.

After each round, ask: "How did you know where to go?" Students will describe landmarks, sight lines, and spatial memory.

### Segment 4: Discussion (2 min)

[SHOW: teacher with gathered students at home base]

Wrap up by connecting to orienteering. "In a real orienteering course, you use a map to find your way back to the finish. Today you used your eyes and your memory. That's the same skill, just without the map."

## Reflection Questions (visual: teacher with gathered students)

- How did you know where home base was?
- Was it harder to return from far away? What helped?
- In orienteering, you use a map to get back to the finish. What did you use today?

## Extensions (visual: examples of extension activities)

- Use a different signal (visual only, or varied sounds) to keep students alert
- Time the group and try to improve over several rounds
- Move home base to a new location, then reset and try again
- Students close their eyes at home base, walk to a random spot, then find their way back

## Tips for Success (visual: return to presenter)

1. Use a tall, visible marker for home base. A flag works better than a short cone because students can spot it from across the field.
2. Time the group out loud. The competitive element keeps energy high and gives students a concrete goal.
3. After each round, take 30 seconds to ask "How did you know where to go?" This reflection-in-the-moment builds spatial thinking faster than a long debrief at the end.
