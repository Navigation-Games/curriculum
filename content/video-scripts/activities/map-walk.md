# Video Script: Map Walk

**Duration target:** 4-5 minutes
**Audience:** PE teachers and program leaders

## Opening (visual: title card)

The Map Walk is the first time students use a real orienteering map. You lead them along a planned route, stopping at features so they can match what they see to symbols on the map. It bridges the gap between the simple pattern maps of Geometric-O and independent navigation.

## What You'll Need (visual: materials laid out)

- Orienteering maps of your area (one per student or one per pair)
- A planned walking route through varied features (you need to scout this in advance)

[SHOW: close-up of an orienteering map with the route mentally noted, then a stack of printed maps]

## Learning Goals (visual: goals on screen as bullet list)

- Students learn orienteering map symbols and match them to real features
- Students read an orienteering map and follow a route on it
- Students orient the map using visible features (not just colored cones)
- Students build confidence that the map accurately represents the space

## Setup (visual: aerial/wide shot of the space being set up)

1. Study the orienteering map of your area. Plan a walking route that passes through a variety of features: paths, buildings, fences, vegetation boundaries, open areas.
[SHOW: teacher studying the map indoors, tracing a route with a finger]

2. Choose 5-8 stops where a clear, identifiable feature is visible and shown on the map.
[SHOW: teacher walking the route, pausing at a fence junction, a building corner, a path intersection]

3. At each stop, know which symbol corresponds to the feature so you can point it out confidently.
[SHOW: close-up of map with a symbol circled, then the real feature it represents]

This activity requires no cones, no checkpoints, and no clue sheets. Just maps and a route.

## How to Run It (visual: demonstration with students)

1. Hand out maps. Orient the map together at the starting location.
[SHOW: teacher handing out maps, then holding one up and rotating it to match the surroundings]

2. Teach the thumb technique: "Keep your thumb on the map where you are right now. Slide it along as we walk."
[SHOW: close-up of a student's thumb on the map at the start location]

3. Walk to the first stop. At the stop, ask: "What feature are we standing at?"
[SHOW: group walking together, stopping at a fence. Students look around and answer]

4. Show the symbol on the map. Ask: "What else uses that same symbol nearby?"
[SHOW: teacher pointing at the map symbol, students scanning the map for other instances]

5. Continue to each stop. At each one:
   - Students orient their maps
   - Identify the feature and its symbol
   - Look around for other features they can see on the map
   - Ask directional questions: "If we were going to walk to that tree, which direction would we go?"
[SHOW: montage of 2-3 stops, students pointing at features and their map symbols]

6. At the last stop, ask students to lead the group back. "Who can read the map and take us back to where we started?"
[SHOW: a student stepping forward, holding the map, and leading the group back along the route]

## Progression (visual: side-by-side comparison of basic vs advanced)

- **First Map Walk**: teacher leads, frequent stops, basic symbols only (paths, buildings, open areas).
[SHOW: teacher at the front, stopping often, group closely following]

- **Second Map Walk**: students take turns leading the group to the next stop.
[SHOW: different students each leading a segment]

- **Small groups**: groups of 3-4 follow a route independently, stopping to identify features.
[SHOW: small groups splitting off, each with a map, navigating on their own]

- **Individual**: students walk a route on their own and list the features they passed.
[SHOW: single student walking with a map, writing on a notecard]

## Common Mistakes (visual: split screen correct vs incorrect)

1. **Not using the thumb technique.** Students who do not anchor their thumb on their current location lose track of where they are on the map almost immediately. Remind them at every stop: "Where is your thumb?"
[SHOW: left side, student holding map loosely, looking lost. Right side, student with thumb planted on the map, confidently pointing at a feature]

2. **Making the route too long.** Fifteen to twenty minutes is plenty. Students will be eager to navigate on their own. A long, tiring walk kills that energy.
[SHOW: left side, tired students dragging their feet on a long walk. Right side, engaged students at a well-paced walk]

3. **Choosing a route with no surprises.** Include at least one feature students would not expect to see on a map: a ditch, a small boulder, a vegetation boundary. These moments build trust in the map.
[SHOW: left side, students walking past only paths and buildings. Right side, student discovering a ditch on the map and then seeing it in real life, looking impressed]

## Wrap-up (visual: return to presenter)

The Map Walk builds confidence. Students learn that the map is trustworthy, that every symbol matches something real. Keep it short, use the thumb technique, and include a surprise or two. Once students can track their location on a real map, they are ready for Score-O and independent navigation.
