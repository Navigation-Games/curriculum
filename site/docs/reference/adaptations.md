# Adapting for All Learners

Orienteering is an adaptable activity. The core task, finding things using a map, is largely non-verbal, self-paced, and scales from a single cone in a gym to a forest course. This page collects adaptations for specific needs. Most are small changes to activities you are already running.

Two principles run through all of them:

- **Adapt the task, not the goal.** Every student can experience finding a checkpoint on their own. Change the map, the terrain, the pace, or the support, but keep the moment of independent success.
- **Pair thoughtfully.** The buddy system is a safety practice and an inclusion practice. Partners with different strengths support each other, and roles (Navigator and Checker) give everyone a real job.

## Teaching in adapted PE

Adapted PE teachers work in many settings: one-on-one, small groups, a separate adapted PE class, or alongside the general PE teacher in an inclusive class. The same activities work in all of them.

Start in places your students already know. Photos of spots in the school (the library door, the water fountain) make a good first checkpoint set, and a simple map of the gym or a hallway they use every day can come before the field or playground.

Repeat more than you would with a general PE class. Running Animal-O several times, with the animals moved to new places, builds confidence before anything new is added.

If your students also take general PE, plan with that teacher. When students try an activity in a small group first, they can then play it with their classmates in general PE, already knowing what to do.

Orienteering practices skills that often appear as IEP goals, such as following multi-step directions and working with a partner.

## Developmental delays

Start with activities that do not involve a map: Boundary Run, Gathering, Explore & Find. When you introduce the map, simplify both the map and the task. Use very short courses with large, obvious features. Photo-orienteering works well: students match a photo of a location instead of reading map symbols. Pair with a buddy, and focus on the joy of finding rather than navigation complexity.

## Autism spectrum

Orienteering can be a good fit: it is structured and rule-based, it offers time in nature, and it can be done at a student's own pace with low social pressure. Provide a clear, predictable routine, and offer a preview of the course or space before the activity begins. Some students on the spectrum become skilled navigators; the logical, systematic nature of map reading can be deeply engaging.

## Mobility challenges, including wheelchair users

Design courses on paved paths or accessible terrain, and place checkpoints at accessible heights. Indoor and schoolyard activities (Geometric-O, Basketball-O) already run on flat surfaces. For a dedicated format, look at **[Trail-O](https://orienteering.sport/trailo/)**, a competitive orienteering discipline designed for participants with mobility limitations: navigation decisions are made from observation points rather than by traveling through rough terrain.

## Hearing loss

The activity of orienteering itself is visual and physical, and does not require hearing. Make all instructions visual: written, demonstrated, or signed. Adapt the signal protocols: pair the whistle or verbal gathering signal with a visual one (raised flag or hand).

## Vision impairment

Maria Lepore-Stevens, a physical educator at Camp Abilities (a camp for children with visual impairments), builds orienteering with tactile maps made from puff paint, pipe cleaners, and textured materials, so students read the map by touch. Checkpoints carry physical and auditory cues, navigation leans on sound and underfoot texture, and smartphone compass apps with accessibility features give auditory directions. Grouping students with varying levels of vision lets them support each other.

## English language learners

Orienteering works well for ELL students because the core activity is largely non-verbal: pictures, symbols, and maps instead of text. Use visual instructions and demonstrations, pair students with a buddy who can help translate, and let the activity itself do the teaching. The spatial vocabulary that comes up naturally (next to, between, closest) is useful language practice.
