# Video Script: Boundary Run

**Duration target:** 3-4 minutes
**Audience:** PE teachers and program leaders

## Opening (visual: title card)

The Boundary Run is the first thing you do in any orienteering lesson. Students follow you around the edge of the play area so they learn where they can and cannot go. It takes five minutes and prevents every "where am I allowed to go?" question for the rest of class.

## What You'll Need (visual: materials laid out)

- A play area with identifiable edges (gym walls, fence lines, sidewalks, tree lines)
- Cones (optional, for marking boundaries where natural edges are unclear)
- Whistle (optional)

## Learning Goals (visual: goals on screen as bullet list)

- Students identify the boundary of the play area after following the leader around it
- Students tell whether landmarks and locations are inside or outside the boundary
- Students recognize areas within the boundary that are still off limits (gardens, parking lots, equipment storage)
- Students explain why boundaries matter for safety and respect

## Setup (visual: aerial/wide shot of the space being set up)

Walk the space before students arrive. Identify the boundary you want to use. If any edges are ambiguous, place cones to mark them. Note any areas inside the boundary that should still be off limits: gardens, parked cars, construction zones, playground equipment.

[SHOW: teacher walking the perimeter, placing a cone at one unclear corner]

That is all the setup. No materials to prepare, no maps to print.

## How to Run It (visual: demonstration with students)

1. Gather students at a starting point near one edge of the boundary.
[SHOW: students clustered around the teacher]

2. Explain the two rules: everyone follows you, and no one goes in front of you. Frame it as follow-the-leader.
[SHOW: teacher speaking to the group, gesturing along the boundary line]

3. Run the boundary at a pace the whole group can handle. Point out key landmarks as you pass them: "This fence is our boundary on this side." "See this garden? That is inside the boundary but off limits."
[SHOW: the group running together along the perimeter, teacher pointing at landmarks. Camera follows from a wide angle]

4. When you return to the start, quiz students. Point to a landmark and ask: "Inside or outside?"
[SHOW: teacher pointing to a tree, students calling out "Inside!"]

5. Point to an off-limits area like a garden or parking lot. Ask: "This is inside the boundary. Should we go there?" Wait for "No!" and ask them why.
[SHOW: teacher pointing at a garden bed, students responding]

6. Ask the big question: "So why do we have boundaries?" Guide toward safety and respect.
[SHOW: close-up of teacher leading the discussion]

## Progression (visual: side-by-side comparison of basic vs advanced)

- **Basic**: follow the leader around the boundary at a comfortable pace, then do the inside/outside quiz.
[SHOW: group jogging at an easy pace]

- **Add movement variety**: repeat the boundary with different movements. Skip, gallop, or hop along the edge.
[SHOW: students skipping along the boundary]

- **Add speed**: challenge the group to complete the boundary faster.
[SHOW: group running at a quicker pace]

- **Extend the discussion**: connect boundaries to everyday life. Fences, curbs, signs. Personal boundaries, emotional boundaries.
[SHOW: teacher leading a brief seated discussion]

## Common Mistakes (visual: split screen correct vs incorrect)

1. **Going too fast.** If you run your pace instead of the group's pace, slower students fall behind and miss the boundary. Match the slowest runner. You can always repeat it faster as a progression.
[SHOW: left side, teacher way ahead of stragglers. Right side, teacher at the back of a tight group]

2. **Skipping the quiz.** Running the boundary without the follow-up questions means students passively jogged but did not actively learn the boundary. The quiz is where the learning happens.
[SHOW: left side, teacher immediately moves to next activity. Right side, teacher pointing and quizzing]

3. **Vague boundaries.** If edges are unclear and you do not mark them, students will test them all class. Place cones at ambiguous spots.
[SHOW: left side, students wandering past an unmarked edge. Right side, cones clearly marking the corner]

## Wrap-up (visual: return to presenter)

The Boundary Run takes five minutes and sets the tone for everything else. Once students know the boundary, they can move freely without constant supervision about where they are allowed to go. Do it first, every time, in every new space.
