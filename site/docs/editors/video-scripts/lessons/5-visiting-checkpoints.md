# Video Script: Lesson 5 - Visiting Checkpoints

**Duration target:** 6-7 minutes
**Audience:** PE teachers

## Opening (visual: title card)

In Lesson 5, students navigate with a real orienteering map on their own for the first time. After a Map Walk refresher, they do Score-O: visit as many checkpoints as you can within the time limit, in any order you choose. This is the first activity where students have full control over their route.

## What You'll Need (visual: materials laid out)

- Orienteering maps with checkpoint circles marked
- Checkpoint markers (cones or flags) with letter codes
- An all-checkpoints map (for teacher reference and checkpoint placement)
- Scorecards and pencils
- A schoolyard or local park with real features for map symbols

Before class: place checkpoints according to the all-checkpoints map.

## Learning Goals (visual: goals on screen)

### Orienteering Goals
- Understand what an orienteering map is and how it differs from a pattern map
- Learn map symbols and match them to real features
- Walk a route while reading the map
- Make route choices based on the map
- Visit as many checkpoints as possible within a time limit

### PE Standards (SHAPE America, 2024)
- Applies movement concepts and strategies for safe movement within dynamic environments (2.5.1)
- Demonstrates problem-solving strategies in a variety of games/activities (2.5.5)
- Uses communication skills to negotiate roles and responsibilities in a physical activity setting (3.5.2)
- Demonstrates safe behaviors independently with limited reminders (3.5.4)
- Explains the rationale for one's choices related to physical activity based on personal interests (4.5.4)

## Lesson Flow (visual: timeline graphic showing segments)

Plan for 30 to 60 minutes. About 3 minutes for the Boundary Run, 5 minutes for the map discussion, 10 to 15 minutes for the Map Walk, and 15 to 30 minutes for Score-O rounds.

## Delivery (visual: demonstration with students)

### Segment 1: Boundary Run (3 min)

[SHOW: teacher jogging the boundary with students in the outdoor space]

Run the boundary of the space. Familiar territory by now, but the area may be new if you've moved from the schoolyard to a park.

### Segment 2: Map Discussion (5 min)

[SHOW: teacher holding up an orienteering map, students gathered around]

Introduce (or revisit) the orienteering map. Compare it to the pattern maps from Lesson 3. "That map was just dots and lines. This map is a picture of the actual space."

[SHOW: close-up of map with teacher pointing to symbols]

Orient the map using real features visible from where you're standing. Point out symbols and what they represent. Ask students to find things on the map that they can see in front of them.

### Segment 3: Map Walk (10-15 min)

[SHOW: teacher walking a route with students, everyone holding maps, stopping to compare map to ground]

Walk a route as a group. At each stop, match symbols on the map to the real features around you. Go slowly. This is a teaching walk, not a race.

### Segment 4: Score-O (15-30 min)

[SHOW: students fanning out across the space with maps]

Hand out maps with checkpoint circles and scorecards. Explain Score-O: "Visit as many checkpoints as you can in the time limit, in any order. At each one, write the letter code on your scorecard. Only correct codes count."

Before starting, discuss strategy: "If you want to visit the most checkpoints, what would your plan be?" Start with nearby ones? Visit clusters together? There is no single best answer.

[SHOW: a pair studying their map, pointing, then heading off in a chosen direction]

Set a short time limit for the first round, about 8 minutes, and start everyone together. Blow the whistle to call everyone back, then count correct codes.

[SHOW: teacher and student checking a scorecard together]

Then run a second round: "Can you beat your score?" Scores almost always go up, and the improvement is the lesson.

## Reflection Questions (visual: teacher with gathered students)

- How is the orienteering map different from the pattern maps we used before?
- Which symbols were easier or harder to remember? Why?
- What strategy did you use to visit the most checkpoints?
- How did you decide which checkpoint to go to next?
- What shortcuts between checkpoints did you find?

## Extensions (visual: examples of extension activities)

- **Poison-O**: wrong checkpoints cost points, which rewards careful map reading
- **Poker-O**: each checkpoint has a playing card; collect the best poker hand
- Challenge students to complete three checkpoints from memory

## Tips for Success (visual: return to presenter)

1. The Map Walk is not optional. Students who skip straight to Score-O will struggle with symbols and orientation. Walk the route together first, even if students are eager to run.
2. Keep the first Score-O short, around 8 minutes, and announce a late penalty (lose a point per minute late) before the start. Short rounds create focus; the penalty keeps the whistle meaningful.
3. Place a few easy checkpoints near the start so every student finds at least one, and watch for students running without reading the map. Redirect them: orient first, then move.
