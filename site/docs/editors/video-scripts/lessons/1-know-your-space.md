# Video Script: Lesson 1 - Know Your Space

**Duration target:** 5-6 minutes
**Audience:** PE teachers

## Opening (visual: title card)

This is Lesson 1 in the orienteering progression: here is where you can go, and there are things to find. Students learn the boundary of the play area, practice returning to the Start on a signal, and explore to find animal checkpoints. Everything that follows builds on this lesson.

## What You'll Need (visual: materials laid out)

- Cones (optional, for marking boundaries)
- Whistle or flag (gathering signal)
- Cone or marker for the Start
- Checkpoints: cones with animal pictures
- A gym, schoolyard, or local park

Before class: walk the space and identify the boundary, place cones if edges are unclear, place a Start marker, and place animal checkpoints around the space.

## Learning Goals (visual: goals on screen)

### Orienteering Goals
- Identify the boundary of the play area
- Tell whether landmarks and locations are inside or outside the boundary
- Know where the Start is from anywhere in the boundary
- Respond quickly to the gathering signal
- Explore a space and find checkpoints within the boundary
- Describe what you found and where it was, using spatial language

### PE Standards (SHAPE America, 2024)
- Combines varied locomotor skills in a variety of practice tasks (1.5.1)
- Applies movement concepts and strategies for safe movement within dynamic environments (2.5.1)
- Demonstrates safe behaviors independently with limited reminders (3.5.4)
- Solves problems independently, with partners, and in small groups (3.5.5)
- Recognizes group challenges through movement (4.5.5)

## Lesson Flow (visual: timeline graphic showing segments)

Plan for 30 to 45 minutes: about 5 minutes for the Boundary Run and quiz, 5 minutes to establish the Start, 10 minutes of Gathering rounds, and 15 to 20 minutes for Explore & Find with reporting back.

## Delivery (visual: demonstration with students)

### Segment 1: Boundary Run (5 min)

[SHOW: teacher jogging with a group of students along the edge of the play area]

Start with the Boundary Run. Run the entire perimeter of the space together as a group, with everyone staying behind you. Point out key landmarks as you go: the big tree, the fence corner, the sidewalk edge.

[SHOW: teacher pointing to various spots while students respond]

Back at the start, quiz students. Point to landmarks and ask, "Inside or outside?" Then point to any off-limits areas that fall inside the boundary: gardens, parking lots, equipment storage. "This is inside our boundary, but should we go there?" Finish by asking why boundaries matter. Guide toward safety and respect.

### Segment 2: Establish the Start (5 min)

[SHOW: teacher placing a flag or tall cone, students walking to corners and edges, then turning and pointing toward it]

Place a flag or tall cone and name it: "This is the Start. In orienteering, you go out and you come back. No matter where you go, you always come back here."

Send students to walk to different spots: corners, edges, behind a tree. From each spot, ask them to point to the Start. Can they point even when they can't see it directly? This builds spatial awareness.

### Segment 3: Gathering (10 min)

[SHOW: students spread out across the space, teacher blows whistle, students run back to the Start]

Introduce the gathering signal. Use both an audible and a visual signal, like a whistle plus a raised hand, so all students can participate. Students move freely within the boundary. On the signal, everyone returns to the Start as fast as they can.

Count the seconds out loud. "That took us 14 seconds. Can you beat that?"

[SHOW: students spread out further, whistle blows, students running back faster]

Repeat several rounds, encouraging students to spread out further each time. Ask between rounds: "What strategies could help the whole group gather more quickly?"

### Segment 4: Explore & Find (15-20 min)

[SHOW: pairs of students discovering cones with animal pictures]

Pair students up. "There are animal checkpoints around the space. With your partner, explore within the boundary to find as many as you can. When you hear the signal, come straight back."

After a few minutes, gather everyone and ask: "Which animals did you find? Where were they?"

[SHOW: students gathered at the Start, describing locations while the teacher points toward different areas]

Encourage spatial language: "The elephant was near the fence." "The giraffe was in the far corner by the tree." Build a group picture of the space together. This reporting step matters. It develops the descriptive vocabulary students will use with clue sheets and maps.

Optionally, have a student run out, touch a named animal, and run back. Extend by naming two animals to visit in order. This previews the sequencing in Animal-O.

Send pairs out for a second round. "Can you find more this time? Can you remember where the ones you already saw are?"

## Reflection Questions (visual: teacher with gathered students)

- What does boundary mean? How can crossing the boundary be dangerous?
- How did you know where the Start was?
- Was it harder to return from far away? What helped?
- How many animals did you find? Where were they?
- How did you work with your partner?

## Extensions (visual: examples of extension activities)

- Repeat the boundary faster or with different movements (skipping, galloping)
- **Hot or Cold**: direct a partner to a secret cone using temperature clues
- **Shrinking Boundary Tag**: play tag while the boundary gets smaller
- Move the Start to a new location; reset and find it again
- Move the checkpoints and explore again
- Have students draw a map of the area and checkpoint locations

## Tips for Success (visual: return to presenter)

1. Walk the space before class and place cones where the boundary isn't obvious. Unclear edges get tested all class.
2. Use a tall, visible marker for the Start, and count gathering time out loud. The number to beat keeps energy high.
3. Don't skip the reporting step after Explore & Find. The spatial language students practice there ("near the fence," "between the trees") is the foundation for everything that follows.
