# Our Impact

Navigation Games does not yet have controlled scientific studies of our own programs. What we have is a clear record of what we deliver, directly observable evidence that students learn to orienteer, what teachers and participants tell us, and a growing body of research on the value of the kind of learning our programs provide.

This page lays out each of those. It ends with an invitation: we want to measure our impact better, and we welcome help.

## What we deliver

We have been bringing orienteering to children since 2015, when Navigation Games was founded as a nonprofit in Cambridge, Massachusetts. Since then we have served **over 50,000 participants**. In 2024 alone, we supported **743 classes and events for 14,936 participants**.

Part of our mission is to enable others to teach, so that orienteering outlasts our direct involvement. In 2024, 295 of those classes were run by teachers and partners without Navigation Games staff present.

Most of this work is in Greater Boston, with a focus on communities that have the least access to outdoor learning.

## Students learn to orienteer

This we can see directly. By the end of a progression, students orient a map, match symbols to real features in the world, and navigate a course. These are the measurable skills leading to expertise in the sport. PE teachers adopt the unit because they watch it work.

We also build our lessons to recognized learning standards. When a lesson is designed to meet a [SHAPE America PE standard](/reference/frameworks/pe-standards) and students meet it, that is a documented learning outcome, achieved. Meeting established standards is itself a form of measured impact.

## Learning to orienteer is only the start

Navigating with a map is the visible skill. It is not the only goal. The progression is designed so that, in the course of learning to navigate, students build capacities that matter far beyond the sport:

- **Confidence, especially outdoors.** Each activity has small, achievable steps, so by the time a student navigates a course, they have proof they can orienteer. That confidence carries into unfamiliar places and unfamiliar problems.
- **Setting a goal and working out how to reach it.** Orienteering is planning made physical: decide where you are going, choose a route, commit, and adjust when it does not work. Students run that loop repeatedly with each course they navigate.
- **Problem-solving and resilience.** Route choice means weighing options with incomplete information, making a good choice, and carrying it out. Recovering from a wrong turn without panicking is a skill, and it transfers to any unfamiliar problem.
- **Collaboration and communication.** Partner roles and relays give every student a real job and a real reason to talk, agree, and work through disagreement together.
- **Spatial reasoning.** Translating between a map and the world is sustained practice in spatial thinking, which research links to later success in mathematics and STEM.
- **Connection with nature.** Students spend the session outside, paying close attention to the ground, the trees, and the terrain, often in green spaces near them that they had never really explored.
- **Physical fitness.** Students keep moving for the whole session, often over varied ground.

These are the outcomes we are most after, what the progression is built to produce, and what teachers, camps, and families tell us they see.

## What teachers and participants tell us

After programs, we ask participants what they thought. A few voices, in their words:

> The right amount of info was given, but the fun was to figure the rest out.

> I can't believe that just a few days ago we were orienteering in this small area, and now we know how to explore the whole park!

> It builds skills such as graphical representation, memory, and communication with others.

Teachers often tell us the biggest surprise is engagement. Students who rarely take part in physical education will spend the whole class running around, because orienteering is a fun game they can play at their own pace. One teacher described three students she had been warned would be difficult; by the end they were helping classmates improve their times and leading the group.

After a woods field trip, one student wrote that the high point was "when we started actually going out and orienteering, because I felt like I could actually do it." Another, after getting turned around, said simply, "the key was not to panic." That confidence and composure is among the most transferable things orienteering teaches.

These anecdotes from our program participants show a clear impact on individuals.

## What the research shows

We are fortunate that a large body of research strongly predicts the impact of programs like ours. Our programs combine physical activity, time outdoors, spatial reasoning, problem-solving, and collaboration, and each is well studied and linked to positive outcomes for children.

### Orienteering itself

- **A 2025 meta-analysis** found large effects of orienteering programs on physical fitness and memory in non-athletes, with a medium effect on spatial skills. The authors recommend orienteering for all ages, including in schools (Yen et al., *Public Health*, 2025).
- **Studies with schoolchildren** have linked orienteering programs to better inhibitory control in 7- and 8-year-olds (Ben Chikha et al., *Journal of Sports Research*, 2021), better executive function in fourth graders (*Chinese Journal of School Health*, 2021), better attention and problem-solving in fourth graders with ADHD (Uzuner, *Journal of Educational Leadership and Policy Studies*, 2021), and higher academic achievement and map literacy in a sixth-grade social studies unit (Ayuldeş & Akbaş, *Education and Science*, 2023).
- **In adults,** orienteering combined with vigorous exercise improved cognition and raised brain-derived neurotrophic factor more than exercise alone (Waddington et al., *PLoS ONE*, 2024). Experienced orienteers report stronger spatial processing and memory across adulthood (Waddington & Heisz, *PLoS ONE*, 2023), and they score above normal levels on tests of executive function, attention, and memory, outperforming road runners on spatial working memory (Hohl et al., *Perceptual and Motor Skills*, 2025).

### Navigation and spatial skills

- **Kids keep getting better at mental maps until about age 12.** The ability to build a mental map of a place develops through elementary and middle school and reaches adult levels around 12. Even then, some children put routes together into a full mental map and some never do (Nazareth, Weisberg, Margulis & Newcombe, *Journal of Experimental Child Psychology*, 2018). Our curriculum covers those same grades.
- **Practicing navigation changes the brain.** London trainee taxi drivers who learned the city and passed the licensing test grew more gray matter in the hippocampus, the part of the brain that handles both navigation and memory. Trainees who didn't pass, and non-drivers, did not (Woollett & Maguire, *Current Biology*, 2011).
- **GPS may have the opposite effect.** Adults who relied more on GPS had weaker spatial memory, and in a small three-year follow-up, more GPS use went with a steeper decline (Dahmani & Bohbot, *Scientific Reports*, 2020). In orienteering, kids do the navigating themselves.
- **Spatial skills can be taught.** A meta-analysis of 217 studies found that training improves them, the gains hold up over time and carry over to spatial tasks that weren't trained, and the people who started out weakest improved the most (Uttal, Newcombe, et al., *Psychological Bulletin*, 2013).
- **Spatial skills help with math.** Spatial reasoning predicts later success in mathematics, and teaching spatial skills carries over into math (Geer et al., *Developmental Psychology*, 2019; Lowrie & Logan, *Journal of Intelligence*, 2023). Childhood spatial skills also help explain why fewer women go into STEM fields (Tian, Ren, Newcombe, et al., *Developmental Science*, 2023).

### Independence and the outdoors

In *The Anxious Generation* (2024), Jonathan Haidt calls for more independence, free play, and responsibility in the real world, and for more play in schools. Orienteering does that during the school day. Kids head off on their own with a map and have to find their way back.

- **Kids benefit from time out of sight of adults.** A systematic review found that play where children can get out of sight of adults, or even get lost, went with more physical activity and better social health (Brussoni et al., *International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health*, 2015). It is one of six kinds of "risky play" the researchers recommend supporting.
- **Knowing where you are builds confidence.** Building and trusting a mental map of the world lets children explore with confidence and independence, and eases the stress of not knowing where they are (Bond, *From Here to There*, Harvard University Press, 2021).
- **Learning outside.** A major review of outdoor learning research found that well-planned fieldwork helps students remember what they learned, and that outdoor adventure programs build confidence, self-esteem, and social skills (Rickinson et al., *A Review of Research on Outdoor Learning*, National Foundation for Educational Research, 2004). In a study of 451 children, mostly 8 and 9 years old, those who engaged more with nature reported greater wellbeing (Sheldrake & Reiss, *The Impact of Nature on Children's Wellbeing*, The Wildlife Trusts).

We put our resources into delivering programs and improving the curriculum, informed by this research, our own experience, and feedback from the people we work with. We keep a fuller, sourced review in [Orienteering Education and Research Around the World](https://navigation-games-2.gitbook.io/orienteering-education-around-the-world/literature/introduction-to-the-literature).
