Junction Control: how the simulator works

Junction Control is a team game on a trail network. Teams race to visit every checkpoint, traveling only on trails. The team with the most players at an intersection blocks it, and players who arrive outnumbered are held until the blocking team lets them go. When a team has every checkpoint, its players head for buses around the edge of the map, and the first team with everyone on a bus wins.

The simulator plays the game with dots, so we can try rules and ways of playing before trying them with real people. The school version is the Junction Control activity.

Reading the map

  • Trails are the dashed lines. Players travel only on them.
  • Intersections are the numbered places where three or more trails meet. A bend in a trail (two ways to go) or a dead end (one) isn't an intersection: it has no number, and nobody blocks or is held there.
  • Checkpoints are the purple circles. A small colored dot under a checkpoint shows that team has it.
  • The start is the purple triangle. Nobody can block the start.
  • Bus stops are the dashed boxes on the edge (B1 to B4, or fewer; see Bus stops). The line under the map gives the bus times. Each bus pulls up shortly before it leaves (90 seconds, or less when buses come close together) and drives off along the edge. Players waiting for a bus line up beside the stop, one line per team, and ride away as colored dots in the bus. The map leaves room around the edge so stops, buses and lines stay in view.
  • Players are dots in their team's color. Squares are blockers. A hollow dot is a held player.
  • A blocked intersection has a light circle in the blocking team's color.

The race board on the right shows each team's checkpoints, how many players are held now, the total time its players have been held, and what the team is doing. "What happened" lists the game's events, newest first.

Rules settings

How long a team can hold someone
"No blocking" turns blocking off, so the game is just a race. With 30 seconds, 1 minute or 2 minutes, a held player waits that long, then the blocking team must let them go. With "until they're no longer outnumbered", a held player waits until enough teammates arrive or the other team's players leave.
Who picks the way out
When the hold limit runs out: "The blocking team" sends the held player down whichever trail leads farthest from where they're going. "Back the way they came" sends them back. "The held player" lets them choose.
Who can leave an intersection

Three versions of the rule. In each one, the team with the most players at an intersection blocks it, and a tie goes to the team already blocking. The default is "A lead frees all, a tie freezes all", the rule in the activity.

  • The most, and teams tied with it. If 2 Red are holding 1 Green and a second Green arrives, the count is tied and either Green can leave. Since players leave one at a time, the first Green to go leaves the other outnumbered again. So a rescue here is really a swap.
  • Only the blocking team. A tie isn't enough. To free a held player, teammates have to outnumber the blockers.
  • A lead frees all, a tie freezes all. A team with the most players at an intersection can leave, and its players who are leaving go together, at the same moment, so a rescue gets everyone out. The rest can stay: one player can leave while the others keep blocking. (Until November 11, they left one by one and stayed free as they went, so for a moment a team could leave without having the most.) If someone arrives and ties the count for the most, everyone there is stuck until the count changes or the hold limit runs out. A lone player passing an intersection where two other teams are tied is stuck too.
Players see a block

How much players know about blocks they haven't reached yet.

  • From anywhere: every player always knows every block on the map, as if teammates reported them all, and routes around the ones where they'd be held.
  • From 100 m or 40 m away: a player sees an intersection once they're that close along the trail. Standing at an intersection, they also see neighboring intersections that close.
  • On the last third of the trail: a player sees the intersection ahead once they're two thirds of the way along the trail.
  • Only when they get there.

When a player sees that they'd be held at the intersection ahead, they compare going on (and waiting out a hold) with turning back and going around. If a held teammate is there and their arrival would free them, they go on and rescue. Pairs and packs make the choice together. Players remember what they've seen for 3 minutes, and plan routes around blocks they remember. They don't share what they see with teammates (see Not in the simulator yet).

Checkpoints, Checkpoints are
How many checkpoints, and whether they're at intersections or halfway along trails. At intersections, they go on real intersections first. On a small network without enough intersections, some go at bends and dead ends.
Bus stops, How often buses leave

1 to 4 bus stops: 1 is B1 on the left edge, 2 are on opposite sides, 3 are left, top and right. Fewer stops brings every team past the same few intersections at the end. Checkpoints stay where they are when you change the number of stops.

"One somewhere every 75 s" keeps the total number of buses the same, so one stop gets a bus every 1:15, two stops every 2:30 each, four stops every 5 minutes each. Or choose a bus every 5 or 10 minutes at each stop. The stops take turns, evenly spaced.

With one stop, how often buses leave doesn't matter: getting there sooner never means a later bus, and a tie on the same bus goes to the team whose last player got there first, so the finish order is the order teams arrive. Buses come into their own with two or more stops, where a player chooses which bus to run for.

Wrong turns at an intersection
How often a player takes a different trail than they meant to. They notice at the next intersection and replan.
Time limit
If no team is on a bus by then, the team with the most checkpoints is ahead, but nobody wins.
One player leaves an intersection at a time
Departures from an intersection are 3 seconds apart, so every departure changes the count before the next player goes. Under "a lead frees all", the leading team's players who are leaving go together. With this off, any players allowed to leave can go at the same moment. That matters most under "The most, and teams tied with it": a teammate who ties the count can leave together with the held player, so a rescue frees them instead of swapping. See findings.
Teams send someone to free a held teammate
See Rescues.
Herding
When two teams meet head-on on a trail and one group is bigger, the smaller group turns around and walks back to the intersection it came from (past any bends), then waits there. When the herders arrive, the herded players are outnumbered, so they're held. Groups of the same size pass each other.
Guarding
A player who is held at an intersection doesn't get its checkpoint. They get it only once they're free to leave: when teammates arrive, or the other team leaves. If the hold limit runs out first, they're sent away without it. Guarding also changes what blockers do (see Guarding). It applies to checkpoints at intersections, not ones on trails.
Players route around intersections where they'd be held
With this on, players plan their route with a penalty for each intersection where they know they'd be held (3 times the hold limit, or 10 minutes with no limit), so they go around a block when the detour is shorter than the wait. Which blocks they know about depends on "Players see a block". With limited sight, they also decide whether to turn back when they see a block ahead. With this off, players take the shortest route no matter what's ahead, never turn back, and walk into any block on the way.

Trail network settings

Network size
The number of points across and down. Each point becomes an intersection, a bend or a dead end, depending on how many trails reach it. The line above the map says how many intersections the map has.
Distance between intersections
40 m is schoolyard paths, 80 m school and park, 150 m a park or campus, 250 m forest trails.
Trails
Few, some or many loops. More loops means more intersections and more ways around a block.

Teams and ways of playing

Three teams, or two head to head, with 2 to 8 players each. Each team plays one of these ways:

Everyone alone
Each player goes off on their own.
Pairs
Players go in twos.
Two packs
The team splits in half.
Whole team together
One big group.
Blockers + pairs
One blocker (two, with 5 or more players) parks at the busiest intersection, and the second at the next busiest. The rest go in pairs. "Busiest" means on the most shortest routes between the checkpoints, the start and the bus stops.
Two blockers together + pairs
Two blockers park together at the busiest intersection, and the rest go in pairs. On a network so small that every intersection is the start or a bus stop, blockers play as runners.
Everyone alone, spares guard
Like "Everyone alone", but with guarding on, a player with no checkpoint of their own left goes to guard instead of backing up a teammate. Without guarding, it plays exactly like "Everyone alone".
Who goes for which checkpoint
"Decided as they go": each group takes the nearest checkpoint nobody on its team is going for (see Which checkpoint). "Planned at the start": every team divides the checkpoints among its groups before the start, like the activity's planning time. "Only Red plans" lets you compare a planning team with two that don't.

How the players decide

Speed

Players run 2.5 to 3.4 meters a second. Pairs and packs move at their slowest member's speed.

Which checkpoint

Each group heads for the nearest checkpoint that no other group on its team is going for. When every checkpoint the team still needs has someone headed for it, the group heads for the nearest one anyway, as a backup. That's worth more than it sounds: the split isn't planned, so a backup is often closer than the teammate who claimed it. A team always knows which checkpoints it has, as if it had a team channel.

A team that plans at the start divides the checkpoints among its groups (not its blockers). It starts by cutting them into slices by direction from the start, then moves or swaps checkpoints between groups while that makes the slowest group's route quicker. A route goes from the start to the nearest checkpoint on the list, then the nearest next, and ends at the nearest bus stop, and faster groups get longer routes. The plan is good, not perfect. During the game, each group takes the nearest checkpoint left on its own list. A group that finishes its list helps out, taking the nearest checkpoint a teammate isn't already headed for, and the teammate drops it.

Rescues

When a player is held and one more teammate would free them, the nearest free teammate within 350 meters comes. A rescue is a swap: the held player leaves first, and the rescuer stays in their place. Under "a lead frees all", the rescuer gives the team the most players, so both leave together. A player who swapped in isn't rescued in turn; they wait out the hold. Players who can only see blocks up close become rescuers when they happen on a held teammate they can free.

Once a team has every checkpoint, a held player is all that stands between the team and the bus, so the team sends as many players as it takes to free them, from anywhere, even players waiting at a bus stop (whose bus hasn't left yet). They go only if the team has enough players to free the held one, and only if they'd get there before the hold limit lets the held player go. A rescuer who arrives first waits there for the others. Under "The most, and teams tied with it" the team doesn't do this, because a rescue there is only a swap and someone is still stuck.

Blockers and the bus

Blockers stay at their intersection until every checkpoint the team still needs (two at most) has a teammate headed for it. Then each blocker works out which bus the last of those teammates will catch, and leaves in time to catch a bus no later than that one, with 20 seconds to spare. A blocker who gets to a stop before the team has every checkpoint waits there.

Guarding

With guarding on, teams know which checkpoints the other teams still need, as if the game operator announced it. Once any team is down to 5 checkpoints or fewer, blockers leave their busy intersection to guard a checkpoint. They pick one that the most other teams still need, and that they can reach at least 15 seconds before any player who needs it, so they're there first. Spare guards (in "Everyone alone, spares guard") go to the same checkpoint as their team's other guards.

Players going for a checkpoint they know is guarded don't walk in alone. They wait at the point next to it until enough teammates have gathered to get it (a tie is enough when tied teams can leave), then go in together. They give up waiting after 2 minutes and go anyway.

Being let go

When the hold limit runs out, the held player leaves by the trail the "Who picks the way out" setting gives. A held player waiting their turn to leave (one at a time) is still held, and the hold clock keeps running.

Buses

Once a team has every checkpoint, each player heads for whichever bus they can catch soonest, at any stop. The team finishes when its last player's bus leaves. If two teams finish on buses leaving at the same moment, the team whose last player reached a stop first wins.

Trying the rules over many games

"Run 100 games" plays 100 games with the settings and teams on the page, each on a new random network, and counts wins, buses made, average bus time, checkpoints and time held. Use it to see whether a rule makes the game lopsided, or whether one way of playing always beats the others. With 100 games, a difference of 10 wins or so can be chance.

What the simulator has shown

These come from simulated players following simple rules, not from kids. Real players plan, talk, get tired and try things the simulator never does, so treat these as rough patterns and questions to try on the field. Each result is wins out of the same 150 maps (November 11, 2026). With 150 games, a difference of about 10 wins can be chance.

At the default settings

Three teams of 5, 10 checkpoints at intersections, a 6 × 5 network 150 m apart with some loops, "A lead frees all, a tie freezes all", holds up to 1 minute, players always know every block, rescue on, 4 bus stops, checkpoints decided as they go.   • The rule for who can leave mattered most. When only the blocking team can leave, Two blockers together won twice as often. When tied teams can leave too, a held player's teammate frees them just by evening the count, and blocking mattered least.
  • Fewer bus stops and fewer checkpoints helped blocking a little. With one or two stops, the busiest intersections are on everyone's way to the bus. With fewer checkpoints, a team doesn't need every player running.

Other things we checked

At the defaults unless it says otherwise. Some of these are from earlier versions of the simulator, so their numbers are rougher.

  • Planning the split helped every way of playing. With three teams playing Everyone alone and only Red planning, Red won 64 of 150. Planning teams finished 20 seconds to a minute sooner. Planning didn't make blocking pay by itself: Two blockers together came close to an even share only when the team had players to spare (41 with 6 checkpoints, 42 with one bus stop).
  • Rescues mattered most with no hold limit. They cut time held from 7 or 8 minutes a player to about 1.5, and the games nobody won within the hour from 24 of 150 to none. Letting players leave a bus stop to rescue fixed teams that had every checkpoint but never got everyone onto a bus (60 of 150 games before). When only the blocking team can leave, rescues helped the other teams a lot against Two blockers together (it won 50 with rescue on, 19 with it off). When tied teams can leave, rescue on or off changed little.
  • Leaving one at a time matters most when tied teams can leave. With it off, a rescuer leaves together with the held player instead of swapping in, and Pairs won 52 instead of 29. Under the default rule it changed little, because the leading team leaves together anyway. Leaving together also made blocking a bit weaker than it was when the leading team left one by one (22 wins instead of 35).
  • Routing around blocks saved Everyone alone about 20 seconds a game when players always know every block, and nothing when they see blocks only from 40 m away or closer.
  • Spare players blocking, when checkpoints are decided as they go, cost the team about a minute. Backing up a teammate got the last checkpoints sooner.
  • When blockers leave for the bus changed finish times by about 10 seconds.
  • Guarding helped blocking a little at most. "Everyone alone, spares guard" did no better than "Everyone alone".
  • Fewer buses mostly added waiting and ties: with 2 stops and a bus every 10 minutes at each, most games were won on the tiebreak. With one stop, how often buses leave can't change the finish order.
  • Herding happened 3 to 5 times a game and cost players 5 to 15 seconds each.

Not in the simulator yet

  • Talking by text. Players telling teammates which checkpoints they've got and which intersections are blocked, and by whom. For now a team always knows its checkpoints, and each player knows only the blocks they've seen (or all of them, with "From anywhere").
  • Talk between teams and deals, the game operator's announcements, players leaving the trails, and anyone staying lost for longer than one wrong turn.
