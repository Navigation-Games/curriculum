# Impact and Research: Source Summary

A synthesis of the materials in `background/research_and_impact/`, plus the GitBook literature page, assembled to support the Impact page (`site/docs/about/impact.md`) and future grant/measurement work. Organized by what kind of evidence each piece is, because that distinction matters: we do not have controlled outcome studies of our own programs, and the page should never imply we do.

## 1. What we deliver (participation data — our firmest evidence)

Delivery counts over the years. The year-end newsletters carry the authoritative final annual numbers; earlier docs (Impact Statement, Goals/Track Record) sometimes quote mid-year figures that are lower. Use the newsletter numbers.

- Founded 2015 as a 501(c)(3) in Cambridge, MA (501(c)(3) effective June 3, 2015). EIN 47-3952861. Founder Barb Bryant began volunteering to bring orienteering into schools around 2004. 2025 is the 10th anniversary.
- **2018:** 350 events, 4,998 participants (4,636 children). [2018 Annual Report]
- **2019:** 649 events, 9,300+ participants (8,364 kids); 2,500+ different children; 25 staff; collaborated with 51 teachers; reached 98 educators via workshops. All 533 Cambridge 3rd graders got orienteering in PE.
- **2020 (pandemic):** 258 classes, 3,000+ attendees. No programs March-July; invented online/Zoom lessons (e.g., "draw a map of your home"). Program income covered ~1/3 of expenses.
- **2021:** 954 classes/events, 15,000 participants, 3,000+ different children. 2,500 students (half remote) across 12 Cambridge schools. Biggest year yet at the time.
- **2022:** 335 classes/events, 5,000+ participants. 80% of programs taught or co-staffed by partner teachers/counselors/coaches. 6-class unit formally integrated into Cambridge 3rd-grade curriculum (450+ children). Platinum rating on GuideStar.
- **2023:** 534 classes/events, ~7,000 participants. Added middle and high school curricula; Brookline 7th-grade *math* orienteering unit; 9 educator workshops; 5 visiting resident orienteers (USA, Norway, France, Mozambique).
- **2024:** 743 classes/events, 14,936 participants. **295 classes run without NG staff** (met the goal of teachers running activities independently). 27 MA schools pre-K-12; 19 educator workshops across 6 states; 50 maps used, 16 new; 55 volunteers gave 1,840+ hours; NJROTC Orienteering Championships (56 cadets); Boston Harbor Islands (Georges & Peddocks, 250+ people) via a Boston Harbor innovation grant.
- **2025:** 10th anniversary year (newsletter is image-only, no extractable text; get numbers from Barb).
- **Cumulative since 2015: over 50,000 participants** (Slalom application figure).

The teacher-independence number (295 of 743 classes run without NG staff in 2024) is strong evidence for the "enabling others" mission and for sustainability/scale, distinct from raw reach.

For the public page I used: founded 2015; 50,000+ participants since; 743 classes/events and 14,936 participants in 2024; plus the teacher-independence framing. Barb should confirm current-year numbers before each update.

## 2. What students learn (directly observable)

The most direct, honest claim: by the end of a progression, students orient a map, match symbols to real features, take a compass bearing, and navigate a course independently. This is visible in every session; PE teachers adopt the unit because they see it work. No study needed for this claim.

**Mapping to PE standards is itself impact.** When a lesson is built to and meets SHAPE America grade-level outcomes, a recognized learning outcome has been achieved. Aligning to established standards is a form of demonstrated impact, not just paperwork. (Link the page to `/reference/frameworks/pe-standards`.) This was Barb's explicit point.

## 3. Intended outcomes (the frameworks we use to define impact)

**"Portrait of an Orienteer" outcome categories** (2022 stakeholder workshop, drafted with Tony et al.) — designed to speak to both orienteers and non-orienteer educators:
1. Confidence in unfamiliar situations / confronting unfamiliar problems
2. Teamwork
3. Technical skills (map reading, navigation, agility/running)
4. Social/emotional wellness related to nature

The framing parallel: orienteering as "finding your way in life" — understanding the landscape, choosing tools, having a plan but staying flexible, problem-solving.

**The five C's** (NG framing, via Northeastern Ohio Orienteering Club): Critical Thinking, Creativity, Collaboration, Community, Communication. NG sees these as needed both to compete and to excel academically and at work.

**SEL framing** (2021 newsletter): orienteering is "a rich framework for Social Emotional Learning. Those skills include self awareness, attention to others and the environment, seeing failure and difficulty as feedback, iteration/persistence, goal setting and resilience/flexibility."

**Vision / life skills** (2018 Annual Report): "Teach life skills, teamwork, critical thinking, care of self, and map navigation. Finding multiple routes to a goal and choosing the best one for you. Dealing with getting lost." Grounded in the American Development Model.

**OUSA Orienteering Development Model + Skill Recognition Program.** Curriculum maps onto recognized skill progression; intro + compass ≈ Level 1, full skill development ≈ 80-90% of Level 2.

**Equity as a core value.** Primary focus on Greater Boston (Boston, Cambridge, Somerville). Boston Public Schools: ~46,000 students, 70% low-income, 82% high-needs. Programs address the "nature gap" in access to outdoor learning; adapted programming for diverse abilities and non-English speakers.

## 4. What participants and stakeholders tell us (anecdotal)

These are real but anecdotal: staff stories and stakeholder interviews, not measured outcomes. **Permission caveat:** the 2023 HGSE interviewees consented to be *named in the study findings*. Publishing names on the public site is a different use. For the page I attributed by role only; Barb should confirm before adding any names.

Staff stories (from the Impact Statement, written first-person, semi-anonymized):

- Three boys "warned" to be difficult who became leaders, helping classmates improve their times.
- A CRLS student who used orienteering ("pause, find significant features, identify goals, look for routes") to manage SAT anxiety; another who applied the same to getting "lost" in a math problem.
- A student of color, not fast, often last in relays, whose steady self-possession repeatedly drew the whole team into cheering him on — leadership by being himself.

**Student field-trip reflections** (middle-school woods trips; 2019 survey pasted by Barb, plus aggregated 2009 reflections in "Summary of Past Orienteering Field Trips.md"). These are anonymous student voices, collected as reflection, not for publication, so confirm comfort before using even anonymized. They land directly on the curriculum's own concepts (Being Lost, Growth Mindset, teamwork). Strong examples:
- Competence / growth: "Once we started actually going out and orienteering it was a high point because I felt like I could actually do it." / "when we got our first flag because i felt accomplished."
- Recovery from being lost: "When we got lost in the woods, we backtracked our steps and found out where we went wrong." / "Mud and puddles, and getting lost, the key was not to panic." / "we found a recognizable structure and reoriented our map."
- Teamwork / pacing consensus: "we reached a consensus about our speed" / "we struck the perfect balance of competitive but also relaxed pace."
- Social connection / nature: "I loved working with my group and spending all day in the woods." / "spend time with people I don't get to hang out with at school... a great bonding experience."

Aggregated 2009 reflection data (one junior-high trip, ~45 students): teamwork — 41 said their team worked well, 4 said it did not. High points clustered on finishing, navigating well, and finding controls after a struggle; low points clustered on being lost (25) and physical difficulty (19). Informal and old, but a real example of structured reflection data we have collected.

Recurring curriculum-design signal (not impact): students repeatedly asked to be grouped by competitive vs. casual preference (many suggested a pre-trip survey). Worth noting for field-trip lesson design.

## 5. What research suggests (the literature — about the ingredients, mostly not about us)

Honest framing for the page: there is real orienteering-specific research, including a cluster of studies with school-aged children (executive function, attention, inhibitory control, map literacy, academic achievement; see the GitBook library section below). The peer-reviewed studies listed immediately below happen to be adult/elite samples; the child-focused work is mostly the smaller/single-site studies catalogued in Barb's GitBook review. None of it is on our specific programs or the exact children we serve, so we still lean on the well-studied *ingredients* of what we do, but "largely adults" is an understatement. Do not undersell the child-focused evidence.

**Orienteering and cognition (peer-reviewed):**
- Waddington, E. E., Allison, D. J., Calabrese, E. M., Pekos, C., Lee, A., Walsh, J. J., & Heisz, J. J. (2024). Orienteering combines vigorous-intensity exercise with navigation to improve human cognition and increase brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF). *PLoS ONE*, 19(5), e0303785.
- Waddington, E. E., & Heisz, J. J. (2023). Orienteering experts report more proficient spatial processing and memory across adulthood. *PLoS ONE*, 18(1), e0280435. (Suggests orienteering may be a viable intervention to reduce Alzheimer's risk; "use it or lose it.")
- Eccles, D. W., Walsh, S. E., & Ingledew, D. K. (2006). A grounded theory of expert cognition in orienteering. *Journal of Sport and Exercise Psychology*, 28(4), 505-525. (Decision-making, error management under pressure.)

**Navigation, the brain, and children's shrinking range:**
- Bond, M. S. (2021). *From Here to There: The Art and Science of Finding and Losing Our Way.* Belknap Press / Harvard University Press. (Navigation and memory share the hippocampus; spatial navigation builds confidence and reduces stress; children's "home range" has collapsed.) Summarized by NG staffer Jackson Codd in "From Here to There.md."
- Rissotto, A., & Tonucci, F. (2002). Freedom of movement and environmental knowledge in elementary school children. *Journal of Environmental Psychology*, 22(1-2), 65-77.
- Popular coverage: Medical News Today, "Orienteering: Could this sport help prevent dementia?"; UT Dallas, "Life of Mental Mapping May Build Roadblocks Against Alzheimer's" (2023).

**Orienteering / map activity in education:**
- Kobayashi, T. *Orienteering in Geography Education in Japanese High School.* (Orienteering skills as the basis of map learning and field learning; schoolyard practice gives good learning effect.)
- Jourand, C., Adé, D., Sève, C., Komar, J., & Thouvarecq, R. (2018). Dynamics of student interactions: an empirical study of orienteering lessons in physical education. *Physical Education and Sport Pedagogy*, 23(2), 134-149.
- Raubal, M., & Winter, S. (2002). Enriching wayfinding instructions with local landmarks. In *Geographic Information Science* (pp. 243-259). Springer.

**Physical activity, outdoor learning, personalized learning (the broad base):**
- Donnelly, J. E., & Lambourne, K. (2011). Classroom-based physical activity, cognition, and academic achievement. *Preventive Medicine*, 52(S1), S36-S42.
- Bailey, R., Armour, K., Kirk, D., Jess, M., Pickup, I., & Sandford, R. (2009). The educational benefits claimed for physical education and school sport: An academic review. *Research Papers in Education*, 24(1), 1-27.
- Rickinson, M., Dillon, J., Teamey, K., Morris, M., & Choi, M. (2004). *A review of research on outdoor learning.* NFER. (Outdoor experiential learning improves concentration, motivation, self-efficacy.)
- Pane, J. F., Steiner, E. D., Baird, M. D., & Hamilton, L. S. (2015). *Continued progress: Promising evidence on personalized learning.* RAND.

**Nature and children's wellbeing:**
- Sheldrake, R., & Reiss, M. J. *The impact of nature on children's wellbeing* (The Wildlife Trusts). 451 children (mostly 8-9), pre/post surveys; nature engagement linked to wellbeing, nature connection, and pro-environmental values.

### The GitBook literature library (Barb's own research review)

`navigation-games-2.gitbook.io/orienteering-education-around-the-world` — a substantial literature review Barb built. This is our single richest research source and the most defensible basis for impact claims. Public (no auth). Structure: Main (Orienteering Education and Research Around the World; Navigation Games; Project Adventure; Canada; Key Ideas); Literature (intro; papers/books to read; summary of research articles; news summaries; being outdoors; adventure education; orienteering specifically; child development: sequencing; child development: spatial awareness/reasoning); Educational Resources (map lesson plans/curricula; learning standards). Full text available at `/llms-full.txt`.

Framing questions: value of map skills; sense of place and wellbeing; how to assess navigation skills; how children acquire them across socioeconomic contexts; how to teach them; what interventions affect sense of place.

**Research on being outdoors.** Context stats: US children get ~4-7 min/day unstructured outdoor play vs 7+ hrs screens; British 11-year-olds <30 min/day outside; Ohio preschoolers <23 min. Strife & Downey (2009): limited nature exposure has serious physical/psychological health effects; nature access gives children "increased ability to concentrate, improved academic performance, reduced stress and aggression, reduced obesity risk" (citing Faber Taylor & Kuo 2006; Kellert 2005; Lieberman & Hoody 1998; Louv 2007; Wells & Evans 2003). Chawla (1998, 1999, 2006): early childhood nature experiences shape lifelong environmental attitudes regardless of race/ethnicity/SES. Kuo et al (2017): outdoor lessons raised third-grade engagement.

**Research on adventure education.** Lee & Zhang (2019): systematic review of 11 articles (1976-2018) on adventure education in PE; findings include more physical activity, increased self-concept and behavioral conduct, decreased anxiety/depression, increased group cohesion and SEL, enhanced resilience. Project Adventure: Jerstad (1981) self-concept gains (boys); Quimby (1982) significant affective gains, no gender differences, trainable regular PE teachers; Shaked (1995) no significant self-esteem change (Coopersmith) but individual variation. (Effects vary by gender/individual.)

**Research on orienteering specifically (includes CHILDREN — important).**
- ADHD students: improved attention, metacognitive awareness, problem-solving perception (Turkey; d2 Attention Test, Problem Solving Inventory).
- Learning disabilities: positive impact on self-concept (2021).
- Grades 4-8 general population: improved academic achievement and map literacy (Turkey case-control, 2023).
- Ages 7-8: improved directional relationships and inhibitory control (Stroop Color-Word; J of Sports Research 2021).
- Grade 4: enhanced executive function (More-odd shifting, 1-back, Flanker; Chinese J of School Health 2021).
- Grade 4: improved spatial perception (Spatial Thinking Skill Test; Turkey 2023).
- Beginners: better mental representation of space / sequence memory (Corsi Block-tapping, Star-Butterfly; 2012).
- Lingwood et al (2015): landmark presence affects children's route learning. Becu et al (2023): geometric polarization of space restores allocentric navigation in children and older adults.

**Child development: sequencing.** Sequencing is a foundation for academic achievement. First-grade sequencing-instruction study: treatment groups scored as well or better on 3 standardized reading tests and outscored peers on math concepts. Children's representations of daily activities (backward order by age 5; multiple reference points by 7). Planning: ages 3-5 have remedy plans, only 5yo have adequate prevention plans (NG question: can orienteering lower the age of prevention planning?). Berman & Slobin (1994), "Frog, Where Are You?": narration develops naming (3) → temporal (4) → causal (5) → purposes/outcomes (9). Memory capacity increases with age.

**Child development: spatial awareness and reasoning.** Okamoto et al (2015): spatial reasoning supports math; children arrive with informal spatial understanding to develop. Nardini et al (2008): object-referenced, viewpoint-independent recall emerges ~age 5; core adult spatial competence present by 3. Spelke et al (2010): children reorient by geometry; learning spatial prepositions ("left of/right of") lets them combine geometry with landmarks; teaching the phrases to 4-4.5yo improved reorientation. Geer et al (2019): spatial-math correlation, longitudinal. Mitchell & Malone (2024): embedded spatial visualization in math lessons gave "large additive effects." Nazareth et al (2018): adult navigation proficiency by age 12; perspective-taking predicts improvement; navigator types (imprecise / non-integrators / integrators). Burgess (2008): parallel egocentric (parietal) and allocentric (hippocampal) systems. Boardman (1989): orienteering develops map skills across ability levels; gender differences track allowed exploration range (Hart 1979). Caveat: males average advantage on mental rotation, possibly tied to differential outdoor freedom.

**News summaries.** Edelson (ESRI): 2010 NAEP, 58% of 4th-grade teachers use maps weekly but only 21% of 4th graders at grade level (under 10% for African-American and Hispanic students). Edutopia/Becker (2016): outdoor learning builds community, raises standards, increases connection.

**Learning standards.** Maps SHAPE America PE outcomes to orienteering, especially independence/responsibility indicators (e.g., works independently for extended periods; responsible behavior in independent group situations), locomotor skills, movement concepts (pathways, direction, strategy), fitness knowledge.

Caveats for use: several orienteering-specific studies are single studies, small, or case-control (notably several from Turkey and China). Real and child-focused, but not large multi-site RCTs. Represent as "studies have found," not settled consensus.

## 6. The two HGSE qualitative studies of Navigation Games

Both are HGSE EDU S513 qualitative program/policy evaluations. **Qualitative, small-sample, perception-focused — not outcome studies.** Represent accurately.

- **Pielow, G., Snyder, A., & James, R. (2023). Program Evaluation of Navigation Games' Implementation of Orienteering.** Goal: understand impact on K-12 participants in MA, barriers to access, and scaling. Findings organized around: (1) Problem-Solving Skills, (2) Equity, (3) Scalability Challenges, (4) Participant views on shortcomings/improvements. Source of the stakeholder interview CSV.
- **Camarena, A., Cardone, L., Gong, L., & Zeng, C. (2025). A Qualitative Evaluation of Community Perceptions of Navigation Games.** Examined how the national orienteering community perceives NG. Themes: volunteerism/burnout/club sustainability; youth engagement and retention; school integration and educational potential. Notable interviewee line: "You came for the map, but you stayed for these people." Recommendation 1: co-develop a mentorship program connecting kids to local OUSA clubs. Honest about its own limitation: homogeneous (mostly white, male, adult) sample.

These show real academic interest and give us a literature review and recommendations, but neither measures student outcomes quantitatively.

## 7. How we measure now, and how we want to measure better

Current measurement (from Slalom Q19):
- Participation data (programs, students, families, educators, demographics)
- Skills assessment (map-navigation task completion; collaboration in team activities; PE standards alignment)
- Participant feedback (pre/post student surveys on map skills, environmental attitudes, social interaction, confidence; teacher evaluations)
- Partner feedback (land managers, schools, nonprofits)

Aspirations and groundwork:
- Federal standards of evidence we'd like to climb toward: ESSA Tiers 1-4 and the What Works Clearinghouse (WWC) Standards Handbook v4.1. We are realistically at the logic-model / correlational end and want to move up.
- Collaboration with HGSE classes (2023, 2025) on program/policy evaluation.
- Conversations with researcher Emma Waddington (orienteering + cognition): possible pre/post designs, validated instruments (growth mindset, self-efficacy, sense of social fit scale), kids vs. adults, "use it or lose it," consent language in school agreements, thematic analysis of open-ended survey responses. "Start small. Even one super solid outcome is tangible."
- Conversation with Rachel Gakenheimer (HGSE) on internships/evaluation capacity.
- Assessment tools noted: self-esteem, growth mindset, self-efficacy, sense of social fit, patterns of adaptive learning, achievement-goal questionnaire. IRB / human-subjects training would be needed for formal research.

**The page should welcome ideas from others on better measurement** (Barb's explicit request) — researchers, schools, and camps invited to help design rigorous outcome measurement. This turns the honesty into an invitation.
